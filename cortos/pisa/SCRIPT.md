## Guion en español (v3, el que se graba; ~60 s)
La Torre de Pisa sigue en pie solo porque su ciudad no paraba de ir a la guerra.
En 1173, Pisa construye un campanario de mármol sobre apenas tres metros de cimientos, en suelo blando.
Cinco años después, en el segundo piso, la torre empieza a hundirse de un lado.
Entonces Pisa se va a la guerra, y la torre queda abandonada casi cien años.
Resulta que el suelo blando es como la arena mojada: te hundís, pero si te quedás quieto, el agua se escurre y la arena se compacta.
Un siglo de guerra compactó el suelo. Sin eso, la torre se caía.
Cuando vuelven, hacen cada piso más alto del lado que se hunde. Por eso la torre está curvada, como una banana.
En 1934, Mussolini manda meterle toneladas de cemento en la base para enderezarla.
Por suerte, no se cayó. Por desgracia, se inclinó todavía más.
En 1990, los ingenieros sacan tierra de abajo del lado alto, y la torre se endereza un poco.
Podrían haberla enderezado del todo, pero frenaron a propósito. Nadie cruza el mundo para ver la Torre Derecha de Pisa.

## Facts (sources)
- Foundations laid 9 Aug 1173; shallow 3 m foundation in weak, soft subsoil (clay/sand). Began to sink in 1178,
  after construction reached the second floor. (Wikipedia, Leaning Tower of Pisa)
- Work stopped for almost a century because Pisa was almost constantly at war with Genoa, Lucca and Florence.
  The pause let the soil settle; otherwise the tower "would almost certainly have toppled". Work resumed 1272. (Wikipedia)
- Upper floors were built with one side taller than the other to compensate, so the tower is curved. (Wikipedia)
- 1934: under Mussolini, holes were drilled in the base and grout/concrete pumped in; the tower lurched further
  south. Tonnage varies by source (80-200 t), so the script says only "tons". (History.com "Leaning tower needs help";
  Mental Floss "13 straight facts"; PBS NOVA)
- Tilt reached 5.5° by 1990, closed 7 Jan 1990. Engineers removed ~38 m³ of soil from under the raised end, plus
  870 t of lead counterweights; tilt reduced by ~45 cm to ~3.97°; reopened 15 Dec 2001; declared stable for
  200-300 years. The goal was a safer angle, never a straight tower (the lean was deemed important for tourism). (Wikipedia)

## Script in English (v1, alternate; 251 words, ~68 s at 3.7 words/s)
The Leaning Tower of Pisa is only standing today because its city kept going to war.
It's 1173, and Pisa is building a marble bell tower to show off its wealth.
The builders dig a foundation just three meters deep, straight into soft clay and sand.
Five years later, they reach the second floor, and the tower starts sinking to one side.
Then Pisa goes to war with Genoa, Lucca and Florence, and the half-built tower is left alone for almost a hundred years.
You see, soft ground works like wet sand at the beach.
Step on it and your heels sink. Stand still long enough, and the water squeezes out, the sand packs tight, and you stop.
A century of war gave the ground time to pack tight. Without it, the tower would have toppled.
When the builders finally come back, it's still leaning, so they make every new floor a little taller on the sinking side.
That's why the tower isn't just tilted. It's bent, like a banana.
In 1934, Mussolini decides a leaning tower is embarrassing, so workers drill hundreds of holes in its base and pump in tons of concrete.
Luckily, the tower didn't fall. Unluckily, it leaned even further.
By 1990 it's leaning five and a half degrees, so engineers dig soil out from under the high side, and it slowly settles back.
They could have straightened it completely, but they stopped on purpose. Nobody flies across the world to see the Straight Tower of Pisa.
